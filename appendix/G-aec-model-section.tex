\chapter{A model artifact-evaluation section}
\label{app:aec-model}

Chapter~\ref{ch:repro} argues that artifact evaluation is where an intrinsic identifier earns
its keep: a reviewer can check an artifact offline, in under a minute, without asking anyone's
permission. This appendix is the operational form of that argument --- text a programme chair
can lift into a call for artifacts, and the short procedures behind it.

It is written to be \emph{additive}. Every venue that runs artifact evaluation already has a
deposit rule, a badge vocabulary, and a reviewing workload it is trying not to increase. Nothing
below removes a DOI, displaces Zenodo, or asks a committee to retrain its reviewers. It fills a
slot that most guidelines already contain and few can currently fill.

It states \emph{what} must be provided and \emph{what} is checked --- not the incantations. Tools
drift faster than principles, so the operational detail lives in two maintained pages outside this
book. Software Heritage keeps the general checklist for archiving and referencing code --- what a
public repository should contain, every archival route, and how to pull an identifier off the
\emph{Permalinks} tab --- at
\url{https://www.softwareheritage.org/how-to-archive-reference-code/}; that is the address to put
in a call for artifacts. What it does not cover, because it is specific to evaluation, is carried
by a companion \textsc{howto} versioned alongside these notes
(\texttt{docs/HOWTO-artifact-swhid.md},
\url{https://github.com/rdicosmo/source-code-of-science/blob/main/docs/HOWTO-artifact-swhid.md}):
the clean-tree invariant, the working-copy hazard, the self-checks, submodules and \textsc{lfs},
and what to do when two parties arrive at different numbers. Both are referenced by address rather
than pinned to a revision --- they are expected to move as tooling does, which is the entire point
of keeping them out of this appendix.

\section{The slot that is already there}

The ACM Artifact Review and Badging policy asks, for the \emph{Artifacts Available} badge, that
authors provide \enquote{a DOI or link to this repository along with a unique identifier for the
object}. Two slots. The first is routinely filled with a DOI; the second is usually left as a bare
repository URL --- which is a location, not an identifier of an object. Many venues inherit this
sentence verbatim, and a good number have quietly dropped its second half.

\takeaway{Most guidelines already ask for an identifier of the object. Few can name one.}

Filling that second slot with a \textsc{swhid} gives the record something it has never had: a
claim about the artifact that anyone can check rather than trust --- and, as
Section~\ref{sec:aec-reviewers} argues, that nobody at review time then needs to. Everything else
in this appendix follows from that one substitution.

\section{The common case: archive, then name the version}
\label{sec:aec-common}

Most venues run artifact evaluation \emph{after} the paper has been accepted. The authors are
known to the committee, and the artifact lives in a public repository on a public forge. In that
setting --- which is the common one, and the one to describe first --- the entire author-side
procedure is three steps, and involves no local tooling at all.

\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Make sure the version is archived.} The least error-prone route is the
        \textsc{updateswh} browser extension (Firefox, Chrome, Edge;
        \url{https://www.softwareheritage.org/browser-extensions/}). On any repository page of a
        supported forge it shows a coloured tab --- green if the archive is already up to date,
        yellow if it has fallen behind --- and one click either opens the archived version or
        requests archival of the page you are looking at. Nothing is typed, so nothing can be
        mistyped, and you can see whether a save is needed at all. Failing that, paste the
        repository URL by hand at \url{https://save.softwareheritage.org} (no account needed), or
        wire a forge webhook to the archive --- Software Heritage recommends firing it on branch,
        tag or release creation rather than on every push. All the routes, and what a
        well-formed repository should contain before you archive it, are on
        \url{https://www.softwareheritage.org/how-to-archive-reference-code/}.
  \item \textbf{Take the identifier from the archive.} Browse to the exact version, open the
        \emph{Permalinks} tab, enable the \texttt{origin}, \texttt{visit} and \texttt{anchor}
        qualifiers, and copy the \emph{qualified} \texttt{dir} \textsc{swhid}.
  \item \textbf{Hand it over.} Put that string in the artifact appendix, or give it to the chair
        or editor with the submission.
\end{enumerate}

That is all. Nothing was computed, nothing was installed, nothing was deposited a second time.
The reviewer pastes the identifier into any browser and is looking at the exact tree the authors
named, together with the repository it came from, the visit that captured it and the commit it
belongs to (Figure~\ref{fig:aec-core-vs-qualified}\,a). The artifact has also, as a side effect,
been preserved --- which is the thing the \emph{Available} badge was asking for in the first place.

\takeaway{In the common case the author computes nothing: the archive already holds the number.}

\section{When review is blind}
\label{sec:aec-blind}

A qualified identifier names the origin, so it cannot go into an anonymous submission as it
stands. But the qualifiers are exactly that --- a suffix:

\begin{quote}\footnotesize
\texttt{swh:1:dir:2dc0f462d191524530f5612d2935851505af41dd}\\
\hspace*{1.5em}\emph{core} --- what goes into an anonymized submission\\[3pt]
\texttt{swh:1:dir:2dc0f462d191524530f5612d2935851505af41dd;origin=\dots;anchor=\dots}\\
\hspace*{1.5em}\emph{qualified} --- what goes into the camera-ready
\end{quote}

\noindent The hash is the same in both. It is computed from the content of the tree and from
nothing else: not from an author, a date, a repository name or a commit. A \emph{core} identifier
therefore discloses none of them, and resolving it in the archive shows the contents of the
directory and nothing whatever besides (Figure~\ref{fig:aec-core-vs-qualified}\,b). The ordinary
interfaces offer no route back either: the directory endpoint returns the tree's entries and
nothing else, and the graph traversal that could answer \enquote{which origins contain this
tree?} is not anonymously available. That is a practical obstacle rather than a guarantee --- as
with a submitted tarball, a reviewer who already suspects a particular repository can always
fetch it and compare. What the identifier \emph{itself} discloses is nothing, and that is what
the venue is relying on.

So the blind procedure is the common one with its last step trimmed. Archive as usual, take the
qualified identifier, and give the reviewers only the part before the first semicolon. On
acceptance, restore the suffix in the camera-ready copy. The hash the committee checked and the
hash in the published paper are then the same string --- the difference between them is only how
much context the reader is offered.

\takeaway{Qualifiers are a suffix. Cut them for review, restore them at camera-ready: same hash.}

One residual disclosure is worth naming, because it is not quite zero. Requesting archival adds a
row --- repository URL, date, status --- to the archive's publicly browsable list of save
requests. That row says a repository was archived on a date; it says nothing about a paper, a
venue or a submission, and a repository already public on a forge gives away no more by being
archived than by existing. Where a venue's threat model is stricter than that, invert the order:
leave the artifact unarchived until acceptance and have authors compute the core identifier
locally, as in Section~\ref{sec:aec-local}.

\section{If you compute it yourself}
\label{sec:aec-local}

Not every artifact sits in a public git repository, and some venues will want a number before
anything is archived. Then the author computes it, and one invariant carries the whole workflow.

The identifier always names a \emph{clean tree}: for a git-managed artifact, the committed tree of
a precise commit; for anything else, the content of the exact bundle submitted, unpacked into an
empty directory. Nobody --- author, reviewer or committee --- ever hashes a live working copy: a
checkout contains \texttt{.git/}, build output and editor droppings, so its hash matches neither
the archive nor the reviewer's tarball, and is not even stable over time on an unchanged commit.
For a git artifact the clean tree's \texttt{dir} \textsc{swhid} is, bit for bit, the hash that
\verb|git rev-parse <commit>^{tree}| already prints --- which is also exactly what Software
Heritage stores.

Hence one recipe, current at press time (maintained version, variants and troubleshooting in the
\textsc{howto}; the local tools in Appendix~\ref{app:cookbook}):

\begin{quote}\footnotesize
\begin{verbatim}
d=$(mktemp -d) && git archive <commit> | tar -x -C "$d"
swh identify --no-filename --type directory "$d"
git rev-parse <commit>^{tree}   # self-check: the two hashes must be equal
\end{verbatim}
\end{quote}

\noindent If the self-check fails, the tree is not clean: diagnose (the \textsc{howto} has the
checklist) rather than submit the number. Where a locally computed value and an archived one both
exist they must coincide, and that coincidence is itself the end-to-end check of the procedure.

\takeaway{When anyone does hash, it is the clean tree --- never the working copy.}

\section{Model text for a call for artifacts}

\begin{callout}{Artifact identification and archival --- text to adapt}
\small
\textbf{Identifying the artifact.} Authors must identify the artifact by its \texttt{dir}
\textsc{swhid} (\emph{SoftWare Hash IDentifier}, ISO/IEC~18670): an identifier derived from the
content of the artifact tree alone, which changes whenever the artifact changes. Request archival
of the repository --- history included --- and take the identifier of the exact version from the
archive's \emph{Permalinks} tab. The procedure is on Software Heritage's own page,
\url{https://www.softwareheritage.org/how-to-archive-reference-code/}: in short, one click on the
\textsc{updateswh} browser extension, or the repository URL pasted at
\url{https://save.softwareheritage.org}; no account is needed for either. Report the identifier
with the submission. A
revised artifact gets a new \textsc{swhid}, reported with the revision; earlier identifiers remain
valid names for the earlier states.

\smallskip
\textbf{Under double-blind review.} Report only the \emph{core} identifier --- the part before
the first semicolon, \texttt{swh:1:dir:\dots} --- omitting the \texttt{origin} and \texttt{anchor}
qualifiers. The core identifier carries no author, origin, date or commit metadata, and resolves
in the archive to the artifact's contents alone, so it is safe in an anonymized submission. At
camera-ready, add the qualifiers back around the identical core hash.

\smallskip
\textbf{In the camera-ready paper.} Cite the \emph{qualified} \textsc{swhid} of the evaluated
version alongside the DOI of any deposit this venue already requires (for example Zenodo). Its
core hash must equal the identifier recorded at the close of evaluation; verify that equality
before submitting. If the artifact was improved after evaluation, list both identifiers, labelled
\enquote{as evaluated} and \enquote{as published}.

\smallskip
\textbf{Artifacts not in a public repository.} Where there is no repository to archive, compute
the identifier from the exact bundle submitted, unpacked into an empty directory, following the
\textsc{howto} accompanying this call --- never from a live working copy. The \textsc{howto}
includes a one-line self-check; if it fails, diagnose before reporting a number.

\smallskip
\textbf{What reviewers do.} Reviewers resolve the identifier, download the artifact from the
archive and evaluate it. \emph{There is no hash for them to check}: the archive retrieves the
object by its hash and cannot return a different one, so re-deriving the identifier they used to
fetch the artifact would test nothing. Where authors supply a bundle rather than an identifier,
reviewers compute the identifier from that bundle per the \textsc{howto}, so that what was
evaluated acquires a name. Should two numbers ever disagree, the \textsc{howto}'s diagnostic
checklist comes first --- the commonest cause is procedural, not a changed artifact --- and only
what survives diagnosis is reported.

\smallskip
\textbf{What the committee does, once.} At camera-ready, confirm that the core hash of the
qualified \textsc{swhid} in the paper equals the identifier recorded at the close of evaluation.
This is the check that makes the badge mean something, and it is one line of work per paper.

\smallskip
\textbf{Scope.} This covers \textbf{source code}, including build recipes --- Dockerfiles,
lockfiles, provisioning and experiment scripts, pinned under the same identifier as the artifact
tree. Datasets, container \emph{images} and large binaries follow this venue's existing
instructions; Software Heritage is a source-code archive, not a data archive.
\end{callout}

\section{The author's checklist}

\begin{enumerate}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item Develop wherever you already develop; nothing changes about your forge, your \textsc{ci}
        or your packaging.
  \item Archive the version you are submitting --- one click in \textsc{updateswh}, or Save Code
        Now by hand, or a forge webhook for good; see
        \url{https://www.softwareheritage.org/how-to-archive-reference-code/} --- and copy the
        qualified \textsc{swhid} from the \emph{Permalinks} tab.
  \item Report it with the submission: the whole string normally, the core hash alone if the
        venue is double-blind.
  \item At each revision during evaluation, report the new one in a line:
        \enquote{revised tree: \texttt{swh:1:dir:\dots}}.
  \item In the camera-ready, cite the qualified \textsc{swhid} beside the \textsc{doi} of any
        deposit, and check its core hash against the one recorded at the close of evaluation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{For reviewers: there is nothing to check}
\label{sec:aec-reviewers}

A reviewer handed a \textsc{swhid} does not verify it. They paste it into the archive, press
\emph{Download} --- the button visible in both panels of
Figure~\ref{fig:aec-core-vs-qualified} --- and evaluate the code that comes back. That is the
whole of it. Their time goes where it should: into running the artifact.

It is worth saying plainly why no check is needed, because committees keep writing one into their
instructions. The archive retrieves the object \emph{by} its hash. Given
\texttt{swh:1:dir:2dc0f462\dots} it can return that tree or nothing at all; there is no way for it
to return some other tree under that name. Asking a reviewer to download an artifact and then
confirm that it hashes to the identifier they used to fetch it tests their \texttt{tar} command,
not the artifact. The guarantee was spent the moment the identifier resolved.

So what does the identifier buy a reviewer? Not a check they perform --- a check they no longer
have to. What they evaluated now has a name, and \emph{later} anyone can confirm that the version
in the published paper is that same thing. The verification is real; it simply does not happen at
the reviewer's desk.

\takeaway{Resolve, download, evaluate. The archive cannot hand back a tree other than the one you asked for.}

\paragraph{The two cases that do involve a computation.} Neither is routine, and neither belongs
in a reviewing form as a default step.

\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{The authors supplied a bundle instead of an identifier.} Then nothing has a name
        yet, and hashing is how one is made: unpack into a fresh, empty directory and compute the
        identifier per the \textsc{howto}. The same path serves a reviewer who would rather not
        consult a service at all --- it needs no account and no network.
  \item \textbf{The chair, at camera-ready.} One line, once: check that the core hash of the
        qualified \textsc{swhid} printed in the paper equals the identifier recorded at the close
        of evaluation. That compares an archive-held object against an independent committee
        record, so unlike the reviewer's non-check it really is one --- and it is the step that
        makes \enquote{the artifact we badged} and \enquote{the artifact a reader can download}
        the same claim.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{If two numbers ever do disagree,} the inequality proves exactly one thing: two
different trees were hashed. It says nothing about how different they are, or on whose side the
error lies, and in practice the commonest cause is procedural --- someone hashed a live working
copy. Run the \textsc{howto}'s diagnostics first and report only what survives them, the way you
would report a build failure: the identifier obtained and the procedure used, never an accusation.

\section{The questions a chair will ask}

\paragraph{We already require a DOI. Why a second identifier?}
They answer different questions. A DOI names a deposit and certifies a landing page; the deposit
behind it can be replaced without the DOI changing, and nothing in the identifier tells you it
did. A \textsc{swhid} \emph{is} the content. That is why both belong in the paper, not one
instead of the other.

\paragraph{Is Software Heritage durable enough to rely on?}
The identifier does not depend on the archive's survival: it can be recomputed from any copy of
the content, by anyone, indefinitely. This is the practical difference between an intrinsic
identifier and a registry entry --- the registry can fail; arithmetic cannot.

\paragraph{Does this cover datasets, containers and models?}
No. It identifies source code, and build recipes pinned with it. Datasets and images keep the
arrangements your venue already has. A proposal that claimed otherwise would be easy to refute
and would deserve to be.

\paragraph{What does it cost the committee?}
One sentence in the call for artifacts and a link to Software Heritage's how-to. No new
infrastructure, no accounts and no service dependency. It costs the reviewers nothing at all ---
it removes a step rather than adding one, since the artifact now arrives from the archive under
the name it will keep. The only new work is the chair's one-line check at camera-ready.
